Before the story

The story at a glance

The Chhaddanta Jataka is No. 514 in the Pali collection of the Buddha's previous lives. In it the Bodhisattva, the future Buddha, is born as an elephant, and the story turns on a grudge carried from one life into the next.

Main characters

  • Chhaddanta: the Bodhisattva, a white elephant king with six tusks, leader of eight thousand elephants.
  • Mahasubhadda: his senior queen.
  • Chullasubhadda: his junior queen. She feels slighted, and is reborn as Subhadda, queen of Benares.
  • Sonuttara: a hunter sent to bring back the tusks. In the Pali text he is an old enemy of the Bodhisattva.
  • The king of Benares: Subhadda's husband, who sends out the hunters.
  • A Paccekabuddha: a solitary enlightened sage, to whom Chullasubhadda offers fruit.

Setting

  • The lake of Chhaddanta in the Himalaya, where the herd lives by a great banyan tree.
  • The royal palace at Benares (Varanasi).

Sequence of events

  1. The elephant king and his two queens
  2. The sal-tree incident and the grudge
  3. Chullasubhadda's prayer and death
  4. Rebirth as queen of Benares; the tusks demanded
  5. The hunter's trap and poisoned arrow
  6. The robe saves the hunter's life
  7. Chhaddanta cuts and gives his tusks
  8. The queen's grief

The storyboard

Eight panels

Concluding message

Anger takes everything; compassion gives everything.

The queen's grudge, carried across a whole lifetime, destroyed the one she had once loved, and then destroyed her too. The elephant, betrayed and dying, answered cruelty with forgiveness and gave freely. Hatred harmed the one who held it; compassion lasted even as he was being killed.

That is why Ajanta's painters gave this story a place on the walls where monks lived and prayed: it shows dana (generosity) and karuna (compassion) at their most complete.

Historical note

Why this story matters at Ajanta

The Jatakas are the stories of the Buddha's previous births. In them the Bodhisattva, sometimes a king and sometimes an animal, perfects virtues such as generosity, patience and compassion. At Ajanta they were the main teaching material on the walls: monks and pilgrims walked around the halls and “read” the paintings as stories.

Painted twice, five centuries apart. The Chhaddanta Jataka is painted in both great phases of Ajanta. In Cave 10, a chaitya hall of the Satavahana period (c. 2nd–1st century BCE), it is among the oldest surviving paintings in India. In Cave 17, a monastery cave made in the late 5th century CE under the Vakataka king Harishena, it appears again in the mature classical style. Setting the two side by side shows how Ajanta painting grew over about 500 years.

Continuous narration. The Cave 17 painter did not use frames. Palace and forest scenes flow into one another across the wall, and the same characters appear several times. This storyboard divides that flow into eight panels, a modern version of the same storytelling idea.

Technique. The murals were painted on a layer of clay mixed with plant fibre, finished with a thin coat of lime. The pigments were minerals: red and yellow ochre, green earth (terre verte), lamp black, lime white and imported lapis lazuli. The illustrations here use the same colours.

Why a monastery chose it. The elephant spares his killer only because he wears the yellow robe. For the monks who lived in these caves, that was a reminder of the respect owed to the robe, and of how much it asked of them. The story also teaches that anger turns back on the angry person.

The caves were rediscovered in 1819 by a British officer, John Smith, and were declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983.
